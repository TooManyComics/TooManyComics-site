Scenarios

Real moments from a collector's life, and how Too Many Comics changes them. These aren't hypotheticals. Every one comes from using the app with a real collection.

The convention table

Thirty years of people rummaging through my boxes

I spent the 90s at comic conventions, on both sides of the table. Not much has changed since: the wall of comics, the boxes and boxes of books to dig through. It's exciting for the first hour. Then it's a line of strangers thumbing through your well-cared-for books, and every flip of a bagged book is a small gamble with its condition.

Too Many Comics generates a kiosk from my inventory: a self-contained, searchable website of my show stock that runs on a laptop with no internet at all. That matters, because convention-hall Wi-Fi is a rumor. Buyers search by title, issue, publisher, or creator, see every cover big, and come to me when they've found something. Nobody rummages. The books stay in their bags, and I stay in the conversation that actually matters: the sale.

Better: I can hand my show inventory to a buyer's own phone. A share file sent from my app lands in their Too Many Comics app as a browsable collection, so they can search my stock from across the hall.

Behind it: the kiosk export is generated by the app: static files, works offline, updates by dropping in a new export. Phone-to-phone sharing rides AirDrop. Your full collection stays private; you choose what the show inventory contains.

The stack and the offer

“I'll give you eighty bucks for these.”

Anyone who has sold at a show knows this moment: a customer hands you a stack of books and an offer, and now you're doing arithmetic in your head with a line forming. What do these add up to? Is that offer fair? And the old answer, price-stickering every book, goes stale the week the market moves.

In Too Many Comics, every book in my show inventory wears a small label: a QR code naming that exact copy, on the bag, never the book. When the stack lands on my table, the app's Scan a Stack feature reads the barcodes in seconds and shows me the asking total, big enough to read upside down. Now the negotiation is informed: accept, counter, or pull a book out of the deal. One tap records the sale. Even a lump-sum counter-offer gets spread across the books, so my records match the cash in my pocket.

And the stale-price-tag problem? Solved by deletion: the labels deliberately carry no price. Prices live in the app, where a re-price updates every book at once, and freshness dates show me which asking prices are due a fresh look before the next show.

Behind it: the app generates label sheets as a PDF for standard Avery stock, so you can print anywhere. Labels scan with any plain QR reader, no network needed, and the same labels power a box audit that puts the inventory back to truth after the show.

The other side of the table

“Would you want to buy these?”

I've been on the other side of that deal too: someone walks up with a stack of comics they want to sell me. Now I'm the one guessing. Is there a key issue hiding in here? What's this stack actually worth? Get it wrong in one direction and I overpay; in the other, I pass on a book I'd have loved to own.

In Too Many Comics I run the stack as an Evaluation scan: photograph the covers, and the AI identifies each book and flags the key issues, the triage that matters most, because one flagged book changes the whole negotiation. A ballpark pass puts an estimate on the stack and totals it. That number is exactly what it says, a ballpark: it tells me what neighborhood the stack lives in, and my own experience makes the informed offer from there. The app keeps the evaluation separate from my collection, so someone else's books never mingle with mine.

And if we shake on it? One tap marks the books purchased and they move into my inventory: photographed, identified, and priced from the moment they became mine.

Behind it: Evaluation mode scans into its own holding area, excluded from your collection and its searches until you buy. Ballpark estimates are honest about being estimates. The app never treats a cover-scan price as gospel, and neither should an offer.

Dealer to dealer

Selling to someone who speaks the same language

Some of the best deals at a show happen between the tables. Another dealer wants a few of my books, we agree on a price, and here's where it gets good: we both run Too Many Comics.

I select the books and send them straight to their phone. AirDrop, done. The books land in their app's Inbox with everything I know about them: photos, metadata, condition, the works. One tap adds them to their inventory, and my records show the sale.

The part that saves the most time is the labels. The barcode on each bag names the book, and the buyer's app takes over the barcode with the transfer: their scanner recognizes those same labels from now on, for their box audits, their show table, their Scan a Stack. No peeling, no relabeling, no re-photographing. The book changed owners; its identity came along.

Behind it: transfers ride the app's own share format, so a book keeps its permanent identity across the handoff. The import even checks for label collisions, so two dealers' labeling systems can meet without stepping on each other.

After the show

Getting the boxes back to the truth

The show ends and the boxes come home lying to you. Books from no-deal stacks got refiled wherever they fit with a line forming. Some books sold. And a convention floor being what it is, maybe a book or two simply walked away. Your inventory still shows last week's tidy world.

Box Audit fixes that fast. Open a box, run the barcode scanner down it, and the app reconciles live as you go: this book belongs in another box, that book is recorded here but never got scanned. Every discrepancy lands in an Inventory Issues list that persists, so nothing depends on your memory of the weekend.

Then you work the list. A misfiled book gets moved to where it belongs, or kept where it is with the record updated to match. A missing book you classify: it turns out it sold, or it walked away. Both are things worth knowing, and they mean different things to your records. And the app is smart about it: a book flagged missing from one box that shows up in a later box's audit resolves itself. A few audits later your inventory is perfect again.

Behind it: the audit reads the same QR labels the show table uses, with a checklist mode for unlabeled books and hand entry by short code. Issues are tagged on the records themselves, so they survive until you resolve them, not until you close a screen.

The listing desk

Selling a few. To make room for more, of course.

I may not think I have too many comics, but I do have some I'd like to sell. Selling used to mean an evening of retyping: titles, conditions, prices, photos, once per book, once per auction site.

Now I pick the books in the app and export an auction pack. AirDrop it to the computer, open it, and there's a listing workbench running as a local web page: every book with its photos right there, asking prices to assign, auction houses to choose, honest condition descriptions already drafted from the grading. When I'm done it generates the eBay upload file, and the photo folders are numbered to match, ready to drag into each listing.

Then the part that keeps me honest: the page exports my changes back, and one tap syncs them into inventory. The For Sale tab shows exactly which books are listed where, so a book that's live at auction can't quietly go back in a box and get sold twice. The app even warns me if I try to sell a book somewhere else while a listing is still up. Stay organized!

Behind it: the pack is self-contained and runs offline in any browser. The phone ships raw book facts and photos; the page holds the marketplace know-how. Listings carry states (pending, live, ending) shown as pills on the For Sale view, so nothing listed is ever forgotten.

The back-issue bin

Wait. Do I already own this?

Every collector knows the moment: you find a book you like, and then comes the question that has ruined a thousand purchases: do I already have this one? Or worse: I know I own an issue of this run… is it this one?

I open the app and search, by typing or by pointing the camera at the cover. The answer is specific: I own this exact printing, and in what condition. Or I own this issue but in a different printing, which is the difference between a $25 book and a $600 book. Or I don't own it, but here's every issue of that run I do have. My entire collection is in my pocket, current, every time.

Behind it: search-by-image identifies the cover with AI, then answers from the inventory on your phone. The lookup never leaves your device, and it distinguishes printings and variants, not just titles.
